Before machine learning, people tried to make AI by writing down everything an expert knows as a set of if/else rules. This was called an **expert system**, and it's basically a giant decision tree written by hand.

## The idea

Find a doctor, lawyer, or engineer. Sit them down. Ask them: "How do you make decisions?" Write every rule they tell you into code. Now the computer is an expert too.

<CodeBlock language="python" title="A tiny medical expert system">
{`def diagnose(symptoms):
    if "fever" in symptoms and "cough" in symptoms:
        if "body_aches" in symptoms:
            return "Likely flu"
        return "Possible cold"
    if "chest_pain" in symptoms and "shortness_of_breath" in symptoms:
        return "Seek emergency care"
    if "sneezing" in symptoms and "runny_nose" in symptoms:
        return "Likely allergies"
    return "No clear diagnosis"

print(diagnose(["fever", "cough", "body_aches"]))
# "Likely flu"`}
</CodeBlock>

This works! For these five rules, it gives reasonable answers. And in the 1980s, expert systems like this were a big deal. Companies spent millions building them for everything from configuring computer orders to diagnosing diseases.

<WebDevAnalogy webDev="A giant switch/case or routing config" ai="Expert system knowledge base">
If you've ever maintained a 500-line routing config or a massive switch statement, you've felt the pain of an expert system. Every new case is another rule you have to write by hand.
</WebDevAnalogy>

## Why it breaks

Add more symptoms. What about "fever" but no "cough"? What about "mild fever" vs "high fever"? What about combinations nobody anticipated?

<CodeBlock language="python" title="The problem grows fast">
{`# 5 symptoms with yes/no → 32 possible combinations
# 10 symptoms → 1,024 combinations
# 20 symptoms → 1,048,576 combinations
# You can't write a rule for every one.`}
</CodeBlock>

Expert systems have three fundamental problems:

**1. Rules contradict each other.** Two experts disagree. Whose rule wins? Now you need rules about which rules take priority — and rules about *those* rules.

**2. Rules can't handle uncertainty.** A patient has 3 out of 5 flu symptoms. Is it flu? The system can't say "70% likely flu" — it either matches a rule or it doesn't.

**3. Rules can't learn.** You see 10,000 new patients. Some of them have patterns no expert thought of. An expert system can't discover those patterns — you have to notice them yourself and write more rules.

<Callout type="info" title="The knowledge bottleneck">
Getting rules out of an expert's head and into code is incredibly slow and expensive. Experts often can't explain their own reasoning — they just "know." Try asking a native speaker to list all the grammar rules they follow. That's the knowledge bottleneck.
</Callout>

## The real lesson

Expert systems aren't bad. They're actually great when:
- The domain is small and well-defined
- Rules rarely change
- You need to explain exactly why a decision was made

Your linter is an expert system. Your form validation is an expert system. Tax software is an expert system. These all work because the rules are finite and knowable.

But for messy, complex, real-world problems — recognizing faces, understanding language, predicting what a customer wants — you can't write enough rules. You need a different approach entirely.

## The breakthrough idea

What if, instead of a person writing rules, the computer could *figure out the rules itself* by looking at examples?

Show it 10,000 emails labeled "spam" or "not spam." Let it find the patterns. That's machine learning — and it's what the next section is all about.

<Callout type="tip" title="Expert systems are still everywhere">
Every time you write `if (user.role === 'admin')`, you're writing a rule-based system. The difference with ML: instead of you defining the rules, the model learns them from data. Modern AI often combines both — hard rules for safety, learned patterns for everything else.
</Callout>
